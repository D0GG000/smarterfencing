SmarterFencing AI Project

About this Project

My name is Jordan Fan and I'm a junior in high school. I’ve been fencing for a few years. While I’m an E-rated fencer, I’ve never quite had a “breakthrough” moment. I’ve always wondered if there was a better way to dissect and improve my own fencing using a data-driven approach.

Where was I landing most of my touches? Which technique was I the most (or least) successful at? Was I being too defensive early in the match?

While I could certainly watch videos of my own fencing, it was a very time intensive, laborious process. I felt certain that there had to be a better way to capture key pieces of data.

That’s why I started smarterfencing.AI. While I initially sought to use it as a tool to analyze and potentially help me improve my own fencing, as I have begun researching more thoroughly and diving deeper into the technology, I realized that I could create an AI system that could help other fencers and coaches better understand performance by revealing patterns that might be hard to see in real time.

On a personal level, I’ve devoted a lot of time to fencing. More recently, I’ve devoted even more time to this project. And while I probably won’t become a world class epeeist, I have found meaning in figuring out how I could give back to the sport that has given me so much. I hope to help the fencing community and make training more insightful, helping athletes grow through clearer, data-driven feedback. Fencing is a niche sport, and this kind of “automatic” data does not currently exist like it does for other more popular sports like boxing or basketball.

Soon, other fencers will be able to upload their own fencing videos. As long as they follow my recommended guidelines for filming, the AI model I’ve created should be able to give the fencer or coach some really valuable data.

How It Works

This project has two key functional processes, with each having its own layers of complexity. The first process was to extract key points from videos that I’ve uploaded. This was done using YOLO, an open-source pose estimation model. The second process was to train the AI model to recognize the type of touch from the keypoints.

Process 1: Using YOLO to Extract Key Points

Using YOLO to extract poses and keypoints of the two fencers is sufficient in most cases, but I faced a few challenges.

  • Keypoint Extraction and Occlusion Issues: A major problem I encountered during this part was occlusion and accurately tracking the two fencers. In epee fencing, a common attack called the fleche involves striking the opponent and running past them before they can counter. The problem that arose when dealing with fleches was one fencer covering up the other. Originally, my first solution was to use the initial directions of the fencers to reidentify the occluded fencer. While this solution might work in a perfect scenario, many fencing videos have people in the background. Ultimately, this caused the program to assign one of the fencer IDs to a non-fencer in the background.
  • Improving Fencer Re-Identification: Realizing that background people are inevitable, I came up with two new hypotheses: Exclude non-fencers by ranking the size of all humans detected by YOLO. This would allow the program to ignore background people due to their smaller area compared to the two main subjects. Save the initial positions of the two largest humans (fencer 1 and fencer 2). This ensures the re-identification system doesn’t confuse the two fencers after occlusions.

While these two solutions seem simple—even compared to the first one—they proved effective when combined, especially in fencing scenarios. In all my testing, this approach worked nearly 100% of the time.

Process 2: Training the AI model

For the second step, I initially looked to YouTube for fencing videos that I could run through the keypoint extractor. However, I found limited success due to many videos having inconsistent frame rates, poor quality, and delayed scoring lights. Some even used edited-in lights that didn’t match real timing.

Because of this, I decided to gather my own data. With the help of my friends who also fence, I was able to film over 5,000 different types of touches.

After annotating these touches and training the model, I ran into my current issue: overfitting. In simple terms, the model became too used to the specific conditions I filmed in.

Next Step

To overcome overfitting, I plan to gather more data under different conditions and adjust my model to include more variance.

For the project’s future development, I hope to use a more complex open-source model using 3D keypoints, which can handle a wider variety of data with greater spatial accuracy—while still only using one camera.
