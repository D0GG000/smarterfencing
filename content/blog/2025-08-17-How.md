## How the System Works

This project is built around **two core functional processes**, each with its own layers of complexity:

- **Extracting keypoints** from fencing videos  
- **Training an AI model** to recognize different types of touches

---

## Process 1: Extracting Keypoints with YOLO

The first step is extracting poses and keypoints for both fencers in a video using **YOLO**, an open-source pose estimation model. While YOLO works well in most scenarios, fencing introduces severa
l unique challenges.

### Keypoint Extraction and Occlusion Issues

A major challenge is **occlusion** — accurately tracking both fencers when one temporarily blocks the other. In épée fencing, a common attack called the *flèche* involves striking the opponent and 
immediately running past them. During this motion, one fencer often completely covers the other in the frame.

My initial approach attempted to re-identify the occluded fencer using the **initial movement direction** of each athlete. While this can work in ideal conditions, real fencing videos often include
 spectators, coaches, or other people in the background. This caused the system to occasionally assign a fencer’s ID to a non-fencer in the background.

### Improving Fencer Re-Identification

After recognizing that background people were unavoidable, I developed two new strategies:

- **Exclude non-fencers by size**  
  Rank all detected humans by bounding-box area so background individuals can be ignored.

- **Preserve initial fencer identities**  
  Store the initial positions of the two largest detected humans (*Fencer 1* and *Fencer 2*) to maintain identity consistency after occlusions.

Individually, these ideas are simple. Combined, they proved highly effective. Across all testing, this approach worked **nearly 100% of the time** in fencing-specific scenarios.

---

## Process 2: Training the AI Model

The second step involves training an AI model to recognize different types of touches based on the extracted keypoints.

Initially, I explored using fencing videos from YouTube. However, many videos suffered from the following issues:

- Inconsistent frame rates  
- Poor video quality  
- Delayed scoring lights  
- Edited or artificial overlays that didn’t match real timing  

Because of these inconsistencies, the data was unreliable for training.

### Building a Custom Dataset

To solve this, I collected my own dataset. With the help of friends who also fence, I filmed and annotated **over 5,000 different types of touches** under controlled conditions.

After training the model, I encountered the current major challenge: **overfitting**. In simple terms, the model became too accustomed to the specific environments, camera angles, and filming condi
tions used during data collection.

Reducing overfitting and improving generalization across different fencing settings is now the next major focus of the project.
